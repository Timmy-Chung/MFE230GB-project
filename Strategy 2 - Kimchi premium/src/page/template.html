Kimchi Premium

Korea's capital controls give bitcoin a second price in won. We ask whether that local premium predicts returns well enough to trade. Over the last five years, October 2021 to September 2026, it does: the long-short earns 14.8% a year after funding and costs. But funding and costs take nearly two-thirds of the price gain, and five years are not enough to call the result significant.

The idea

Coins can flow into Korea. Won cannot flow out.

Korean exchanges trade crypto against the won, and only residents with a real-name bank account can use them. Residents cannot freely send won abroad: remittances above small limits need documents, and banks and customs police transfers linked to crypto arbitrage. So an arbitrageur can buy bitcoin abroad and sell it in Seoul, but cannot easily bring the won home. Korean prices can sit above world prices for weeks. Makarov and Schoar (2020) document these gaps and trace them to capital controls.

The premium works like a shadow exchange rate: dividing bitcoin's won price by its dollar price gives a second won–dollar rate, set by Korean demand for crypto. It is the same economics as the onshore and offshore yuan, or a covered-interest-parity deviation. One asset trades at two prices, and a constraint keeps arbitrage capital from closing the gap.

Why the gap survives

Mechanism. Korean retail demand arrives in bursts: attention, pumps, listing hype. It lifts the Korean price first. Domestic arbitrageurs buy abroad and sell in Seoul, so part of the pressure spills into the world price. When the burst fades, the price pressure should reverse, as flow-driven price pressure does in stocks (Coval and Stafford, 2007). Robustness · Japan placebo

No capital controls, no edge

One robustness test, the one that speaks to the mechanism: run the identical rule on bitbank's yen market in Japan, where money moves freely. Verdict

Trapped capital makes the signal; funding and costs eat most of it

Lesson. Capital controls make the kimchi premium real, and Korean demand shows up in prices worldwide: the coins Seoul overpays for fall back in Seoul and on Binance, and the same rule fails in Japan, where capital moves freely. Over the last five years the long-short earned a year after costs, but funding and trading costs took of the price gain, and the trade breaks even at about per unit traded. The people who can close the gap already do: they hedge on Binance, and the funding they pay for that hedge is the price of joining them. The same theme runs through Strategy 1: crowding sets the price of a trade, so the cost of entry matters as much as the signal.

No look-ahead. The signal uses daily closes up to Monday 00:00 UTC, the moment of trading. The coin premium π needs no exchange rate at all; the aggregate premium uses the latest noon New York rate, which is set before each 00:00 UTC close. The universe, filters and weights use only data available at formation.

Limitations. Upbit's API serves only coins still listed, so coins Upbit later delisted are missing. They typically trade at a Korean discount before delisting, which would put them in our long leg, so the true result is probably worse. USDT is treated as a dollar.
